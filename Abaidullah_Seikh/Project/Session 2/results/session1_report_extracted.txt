Abstract
Financial asset recommenders often have only a few recorded transactions per customer, making it difficult to distinguish
persistent preferences from isolated purchases. This report proposes to compare LLM summaries of transaction histories with
structured features built from exactly the same available FAR-Trans facts. The planned study tests the difference in future
purchase nDCG@10 on the same sparse history users and common asset universe, with ten ranked recommendations per user,
using deterministic text as a control; no recommendation experiments were conducted for Session 1. Its findings would inform
whether a language representation warrants the additional computation and oversight, without confusing purchase prediction
with investment quality.
Index Terms: financial asset recommendation, FAR-Trans, large language models, sparse histories, nDCG@10.

1. Introduction
1.1 Individual research question (RQ1). Does using LLM-generated transaction-history summaries improve nDCG@10 for
predicting future asset purchases compared with structured-history features for FAR-Trans users with sparse histories?
Structured features (A) are my baseline and summaries (C) the intervention. The common outcome is future purchase nDCG@10
for the same users. Following the team discussion, sparse means 1 to 5 Buy transactions in the permitted history; earlier Sell
transactions remain eligible as inputs. This tests history representation, not investment advice.
1.2 Motivation and data context. Inspection of the public FAR-Trans release found 388,048 transactions involving 29,090
customers: 228,913 Buy and 159,135 Sell events. The median number of Buy events per customer across the complete release is
two, which makes brief transaction histories a central concern rather than a rare edge case. These are full period descriptive
statistics, not counts calculated at an evaluation cutoff. The latter must be recomputed using only earlier transactions once the
instructor defines the temporal protocol [1].
1.3 Why history representation matters. Consider an investor who bought one electricity stock. A structured model can record
the stock, its sector, the purchase amount and recency; a language model could express the same evidence as a short thematic
description. That expression may help connect the investor to assets with related attributes, but it could also create a misleading
narrative from a single choice. The test is therefore not whether summaries sound persuasive. It is whether the information
carried by a summary helps rank later purchased assets after the baseline has already received the relevant category, sector,
amount and time information. A narrow sparse history cohort makes this distinction unusually important: the fewer facts
available, the greater the temptation for a language model to infer preferences that the record does not support.
1.4 Research importance
The scientific contribution is to test a specific claim that is often left implicit in work on language based recommendation: that
summarizing scarce behavioral history creates a more useful ranking representation than aggregating those same facts into
structured features. FAR-Trans provides a transaction benchmark and conventional ranking baselines [1], while FLARKO
introduces an LLM approach in the same financial domain [2]. Neither provides the planned controlled A versus C test for a
predefined sparse history cohort using the course purchase metric. A reproducible gain would support the combined summary
and user encoder pipeline under the stated controls. A reliable failure to improve would be equally valuable: it would limit the
case for adding a language model when the structured baseline already encodes the available evidence.
1.5 Business value
The immediate stakeholder is the owner of a financial recommendation product, together with a model risk or compliance
reviewer. They face a practical choice: maintain a transparent structured feature pipeline or invest in generating, refreshing,
checking and serving text summaries. A meaningful gain in future purchase ranking, replicated on matched users and
accompanied by acceptable generation costs, could justify a restricted offline pilot. A null or negative result would avoid
unnecessary infrastructure and review burden. Neither decision licenses investment advice, proves suitability or implies higher
returns; transaction based relevance and profitability can disagree [5].

2.2 Closest work and bounded gap
FLARKO [2] is the nearest same dataset work because it studies financial asset recommendations with an LLM. LangPTune [4]
is the nearest representation methodology because it optimizes language user profiles. Their results cannot resolve the present
question: FLARKO reports preference and profit oriented top three measures under different context conditions, while
LangPTune uses other data and ranking objectives. Across these five inspected sources, I did not find an information matched
comparison of structured transaction features and generated summaries for users selected by one to five earlier Buy events,
evaluated with future purchase nDCG@10. This is a defined evidence gap, not a claim of universal novelty.
2.3 Interpreting the published evidence
The evidence warns against treating natural language as an automatic improvement. In the ACL 2024 condensed list evaluation,
Ramos et al. [3] report nDCG@10 of 0.940 for a Mistral profile model versus 0.941 for matrix factorization on Amazon Movies
and TV, and 0.955 versus 0.960 for the corresponding Llama2 comparison on TripAdvisor. These figures show that editable
language profiles can be competitive without necessarily winning. They do not establish an effect on sparse financial accounts or
on the future purchase labels used here.
The February 2025 arXiv version of Gao et al. [4] includes a Movie and TV comparison where a 512 token language profile
scores NDCG@20 of 0.0494 against 0.0543 for a specified no profile condition. This is version specific evidence and should not
be presented as a result of the later CIKM paper without a matching check. FLARKO [2] likewise uses knowledge graph context
and a Qwen3 based approach, but its reported Pref@3, Prof@3 and Comb@3 metrics cannot be subtracted from the FAR-Trans
nDCG@10 benchmark. Together these papers justify evaluating both a possible benefit and a possible failure, with the objective
and control held constant.
2.4 Alternative designs outside RQ1. Direct LLM reranking changes the ranking stage and its cost; customer profile enrichment introduces
risk and investment capacity attributes that may be predicted, stale or unavailable at the cutoff. Neither isolates the representation of transaction
history. I retain a deterministic text arm (B) to ask whether any gain is due to summarization rather than merely using a text encoder.


3. Method
3.1 Matched representation comparison. I propose a common timestamped fact table F(u,t) for each user u and prediction cutoff t. For the
provisional May 31, 2022 boundary, historical transactions through that date are inputs and June 1 to November 30 purchases are future labels;
this split remains unofficial until the instructor specifies the benchmark. As-of asset metadata joins must likewise exclude later revisions. Arm
A aggregates matched facts into numeric counts, recency, categorical shares and optional amounts. Arm B serializes these facts into
deterministic text. Arm C summarizes exactly the B text using the chosen LLM, without customer profile fields. The eligible assets, item
vectors, ranking function and training labels are shared. Numeric A requires a fitted adapter to the common item space; B and C use the same
frozen text encoder. Hence A versus C is a pipeline comparison, while B versus C better isolates summarization.

4. Experiments: Proposed Protocol, Not Results
4.1 Provisional temporal evaluation. For planning only, the team has proposed May 31, 2022 as the inclusive history cutoff,
with future Buy labels from June 1 through November 30, 2022. These dates are not an instructor-approved split. The official
eligible user set, asset universe, repeat purchase rules and label treatment remain pending; the instructor protocol will override
these assumptions. Users with 1 to 5 Buy events through the cutoff form my proposed sparse cohort; historical Sell events are
retained for feature construction. All arms receive the same available by the cutoff facts and frozen candidate manifest. Fitting
adapters must use only earlier labeled examples whose outcomes precede the evaluation period; neither future purchases nor
revised metadata may enter the inputs.
4.2 Ranking, relevance and primary metric. Each arm returns ten distinct ranked ISINs per eligible user from the same asset
universe using a common cosine ranking rule. Binary relevance is based on assets bought in the future window, and nDCG@10
rewards placing relevant assets earlier. The primary estimate is the mean paired difference C minus A in the sparse cohort. I
propose three adapter seeds (42, 2026, 3407) and customer clustered bootstrap uncertainty for repeated snapshots. B versus C
helps assess whether summaries add value beyond deterministic text. Any second summary model will be a prespecified
robustness check, not a model chosen for its test score. Eligibility counts, zero positive users, candidate failures and repeat
purchases will follow the eventual official rules. No ranking score has been measured.
4.3 Feasibility and cost. A pilot of 100 histories, a US$50 spending ceiling and 48 GPU hours are proposed planning limits, not
measured expenditure. I will first check access, summary fidelity and cost for Astra (gpt-6-astra) and DeepSeek Flash (deepseek-
flash) on development histories [M3], [M1]. I will then commit to one primary Arm C generator before evaluating the test set. If
feasible, the other model will provide a secondary comparison; GLM-5.3 Flash remains an optional further check [M2]. For each
run I will archive the prompt, API identifier, served revision when disclosed, supported settings, documented knowledge cutoff,
tokens, errors, latency and cost. I will cache output by input text and complete model configuration to prevent version mixing. If
the budget is restrictive, I will drop optional comparisons rather than compromise the common evaluation.
4.4 Staged validation and reproducibility. The first step is to test chronology, schema joins and the ten item output on a small
development cohort, including examples where an asset changes metadata or a customer buys the same asset more than once.
The second step is to establish structured and popularity baselines on a development window and fix hyperparameters without
looking at the eventual test period. The third step introduces the deterministic text and generated summary arms and audits
whether their inputs reproduce the common fact table. Only then will paired runs and confidence intervals be calculated on the
instructor’s designated evaluation users. This order keeps model selection separate from the final estimate and makes failures
traceable. At this milestone, only the source records and dataset inspection are available; there are no trained models, future
purchase scores or observed generation costs.
5. Discussion
5.1 Interpreting the main comparison. A positive, reasonably precise paired C minus A estimate would indicate that this
particular summary and encoder pipeline extracts useful signals for the defined sparse history population. It would not by itself
prove that language abstraction caused the gain: a positive C minus B comparison would provide additional evidence that
summarization helps beyond fixed text. A negative C minus A result would favor the structured pipeline on ranking quality and
simplicity, while a wide interval spanning material gains and losses would mean the study is underpowered or unstable. A small
gain may still fail the product owner’s cost, latency or auditability threshold. None of these outcomes establishes better
investment returns [5].
5.2 Threats to validity. With one or two transactions, the input may be too thin to support a meaningful profile. Generated text
can omit a sale, overlook chronology or invent a preference; the resulting score may reflect the encoder or the model’s pretrained
market knowledge rather than the observed customer record. Price information and asset descriptions may be revised after the
cutoff, and some historical Buy events may be reconstructed rather than directly recorded. These risks motivate strict
timestamps, missing value masks, factuality audits and sensitivity checks that exclude ambiguous rows. Shifts in market
conditions, high concentration in a few assets and selective eligibility can also limit generalization. Purchase relevance is a
behavioral endpoint; profitability, suitability and actual product value need different evidence.
5.3 Decision relevance and negative evidence. The experiment will be more useful if its outcome is linked to an explicit
decision rule rather than a single score. The product owner should compare the paired ranking gain with the burden of summary
generation, model version management, refresh frequency and documentation required by compliance reviewers. If C is better
than A but not B, deterministic text may offer most of the benefit with less generative uncertainty. If all three are similar, the
structured baseline remains a credible default. If C is worse, failure analysis should separate poor summary fidelity from scarce
source facts and from a mismatch between the language embedding and financial asset space. These distinctions make an
unfavorable result informative rather than a failed project.
