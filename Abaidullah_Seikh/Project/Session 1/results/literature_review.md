**Research question:** Do LLM-generated transaction-history summaries improve nDCG@10 for predicting future asset purchases compared with structured-history features for FAR-Trans users with sparse histories?

**The inspected evidence does not establish the answer to this exact question.** FAR-Trans supplies the financial dataset and purchase-ranking benchmark; FLARKO demonstrates LLM recommendation on that dataset; UPR and LangPTune supply language-profile methods. ## FAR-Trans anchors the task but leaves sparse-summary effects unmeasured

### Dataset construction matters to what a summary can truthfully say

Sanz-Cruzado, Droukas and McCreadie describe investment opportunities available to Greek investors through one large European financial institution, including stocks, bonds, mutual funds, asset prices, anonymized customers and buy/sell logs from January 2018 to November 2022. Table 1 reports **29,090 customers, 806 unique assets, 321 assets with investments, 703,303 price points, 388,049 transactions, 228,913 acquisitions and 159,136 sales**. The parenthetical “unique” transaction/acquisition counts in that table do not have a sufficiently explicit deduplication key to relabel them as distinct user–item pairs. Their qualified novelty claim concerns a public FAR dataset containing **both prices and investment transactions**, not the absence of all earlier public financial recommendation data. ([§§2.2–3/Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S3.T1))

The released events are **processed and partly reconstructed**, not all untouched observed purchases. The authors remove transactions lacking customer IDs, adjust shares for stock splits and insert cash-in-lieu sales for fractional shares after reverse splits. When end-2022 balances imply sales from pre-existing holdings, they recreate the missing buys at the earliest available price date, usually 2 January 2018. Transactions outside a price series are moved to its nearest covered date; remaining holdings can be closed when a price series ends; monetary values are estimated from adjusted shares and closing prices. Prices themselves are cleaned, euro-converted, split-adjusted and gap-filled. Customer risk/capacity can be estimated rather than questionnaire-observed. ([§3.1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S3.SS1.SSS1); [§3.3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S3.SS3.SSS1); [§3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S3.SS4))

This creates a substantive representation issue: a fluent summary must not turn reconstructed holdings into certain dated purchase decisions or estimated profiles into confirmed preferences. Because some early-dated buys are reconstructed using end-of-release balances, a timestamp-before-cutoff filter alone does not prove contemporaneous observability. This is an **inferred point-in-time validity concern**, not a measured leakage effect. Equal treatment across representations is necessary for comparison, but does not by itself establish historical authenticity. ([Construction basis, §3.3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S3.SS3.SSS1))

### The published benchmark predicts new-to-user acquisitions

At recommendation time `t`, the formal target ranks assets from the available set excluding those the customer **bought before t**. Training uses past prices and transactions, with a binary acquisition matrix rather than graded spending/frequency ratings. The authors evaluate **61 time points**, beginning **1 August 2019**, described as “spaced two weeks apart”; they do not enumerate the complete schedule. Each point uses prior history and **six months of future acquisitions**. Assets purchased in both training and test contribute only to training. Customers must be active in both periods, and assets must have prices throughout the test period, aligning the population for purchase and profitability metrics. This is neither a random 80/20 protocol nor an unrestricted repeat-purchase task. ([§5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S5.SS1); [§5.2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S5.SS2.SSS1))

The stated candidate set is the available universe minus previously purchased assets, subject to test-pricing eligibility. The text does **not** establish a restriction to the 321 globally traded assets, a sampled-negative protocol, or exact per-window candidate counts. **nDCG@10** measures future-acquisition relevance. **ROI@10** is reported as the monthly average return of an equally weighted top-ten portfolio over the six-month horizon; **0.0259 means 2.59% monthly**, not a 2.59% six-month total. The original workshop text does not give the exact monthly conversion equation. Table 2 averages over evaluation time points; detailed within-window weights are unspecified. ([§§5.1–5.2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S5.SS2))

### Eleven methods provide substantial non-LLM benchmarks

The random baseline is accompanied by three price-based ROI regressors: **random forest, linear regression and LightGBM**, using indicators such as average price, past ROI, volatility, MACD, momentum, rate of change, RSI, detrended price oscillator, ROI/volatility and previous-period extrema. Transaction methods are **popularity**, **LightGCN**, **Apriori association-rule mining (ARM)**, **matrix factorization (MF)** and **user-based kNN**. Popularity is described as number of prior purchases; the paper does not fully reconcile event-count popularity with binary acquisition preprocessing. Two hybrids use recommender outputs as features: **LightGBM regression** for profitability and **LambdaMART/Hybrid-nDCG** for ranking. Neither hybrid uses generated text. All algorithms' hyperparameters are selected by **six-month ROI at 1 April 2019, 1 October 2019 and 31 January 2020**, including the model with an nDCG training objective. ([§5.2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S5.SS2.SSS3))

The complete published Table 2 is reproduced below. ROI retains the paper's decimal monthly scale; market/customer rows are references, not additional algorithms. ([§5.3/Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S5.T2))

### Sparsity motivation is stronger than sparse-user effect evidence

The paper states that **over 50% of users have three or fewer transactions over 2018–2022**. That is full-period total activity, not pre-cutoff Buy counts or distinct purchased assets. It reports no sparse-specific nDCG, summary arm, confidence intervals or significance tests for Table 2. Six-month windows at approximately fortnightly origins overlap, and the listed ROI-tuning dates are not all earlier than the first test origin. Exact date lists, seeds, per-window populations, tuning grids and nested chronological validation are not established in the inspected text. These are reproducibility/interpretation limits, not proof of a quantified implementation error. A single institution, concentrated asset popularity and one market period also bound transfer. ([§3.3.2/Fig.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S3.SS3.SSS2); [§5.2–5.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S5.SS2))

### Task1 supplies local context without defining the experimental cohort

The existing local inspection reports **388,048 transaction data rows**, **29,090 customers**, **320 traded ISINs**, **806 metadata ISINs**, and **807 price-series ISINs**. Table 1's transaction/traded-asset counts are **388,049 / 321**. The paper itself also mixes 806 in Table 1 and 807 in §3.2. One header makes the local CSV-record total 388,049, but that arithmetic does **not establish why the paper differs**. Local transaction dates extend to **30 November 2022**, whereas the institutional collection metadata ends **29 November**; prices locally end on 29 November. These distinctions remain unresolved rather than silently harmonized. ([Task1 inventory, “Customers, assets and transactions”](dataset_inventory.md#customers-assets-and-transactions); [Task1 date checks](data_quality_analysis.md#dates-and-numeric-checks); [paper Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08692v1#S3.T1); [institutional dataset record](https://researchdata.gla.ac.uk/1658/))

Task1's full-release median is **three total events and two Buy events**; **21,237 customers (73.00%)** have 1–3 Buys, **23,663 (81.34%)** have 1–5, and **25,736 (88.47%)** have 1–10. These overlapping bands are descriptive only. Moreover, **360** customers in the 1–5 Buy band have more than ten total events, so purchase-sparse does not necessarily mean sparse buy/sell input. Task1's proposed 1–5 pre-cutoff Buy band remains provisional. It cannot be populated by reusing whole-release counts. ([Task1 sparse-user analysis](sparse_user_analysis.md))

Information equivalence must also account for measured metadata quality: among **836 asset metadata rows**, sector is blank in **388** and industry in **439**; **three ISIN/date keys** have conflicting records. Transaction IDs alone repeat, while `(customerID, transactionID)` is unique in the measured release. **12,501 customer-profile rows** have questionnaire dates later than their own snapshot timestamps. These are row/key-level observations, not counts of sparse users. They matter because summary generation can hide missingness, conflate records or add unsupported preference interpretations; the structured comparator should receive the same permitted, time-valid fields. ([Task1 data-quality analysis, missing values, key duplication and timing](data_quality_analysis.md))

## FLARKO supplies same-dataset LLM evidence, not a summary comparison

Spadea and Seneviratne's FLARKO constructs a **personal knowledge graph** with ISIN, buy/sell type, value and timestamp, and a **market knowledge graph** with price summaries and asset category/sector/industry. Both are triples serialized as **JSON-LD**, capped jointly at **5,000 triples**. Ten-week price summaries are numeric aggregations, not LLM-written transaction narratives. Events and completed price-summary periods precede the recommendation date. Qwen3 models receive these graphs and generate three ISINs for the next six months. **KTO** labels desirable recommendations using the intersection of an actual purchase within **180 days** and a positive asset return. The method therefore changes representation, ranking model and behavioral/profitability supervision together. ([arXiv v1, §§3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15993v1#S3))

The study uses FAR-Trans training data from **2 January 2018–30 November 2021**, with prompts every four weeks during **1 August 2019–1 June 2021**. Test data span **1 December 2021–29 November 2022**, with prompts every two weeks during **1 December 2021–2 June 2022** and 180-day outcome buffers. Qwen3 sizes are **0.6B, 1.7B, 4B and 8B**; centralized training uses three epochs, while simulated federated experiments use twenty clients and omit 8B for compute reasons. The **23,784 balanced labeled examples** are training examples, not a reported number of evaluated investors. The conventional baselines include random, price regressors and transaction-based popularity/CF/ARM methods. ([§6.1–6.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15993v1#S6))

Evaluation uses **Pref@3, Prof@3 and Comb@3**, described as Hits@3 against purchased, profitable and intersection sets. No nDCG@10 is reported. Table 2 gives **0.6B/PKG Pref@3 0.4439 ± 0.0355**, compared with **0.6B/Combined 0.0354 ± 0.0131**. The highest centralized preference score is **1.7B/MKG 0.5341 ± 0.0532**; highest profitability score is **4B/Combined 0.6400 ± 0.0554**; highest combined score is **1.7B/Nothing 0.3718 ± 0.0547**. These maxima belong to different configurations. The ± values are the caption's **standard errors of proportions**, not confidence intervals or across-seed standard deviations. ([§6.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15993v1#S6.SS3); [§7.1/Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15993v1#S7.T2))

The paper's broad conclusion that combining personal and market graphs yields the strongest results needs qualification: **Combined is below PKG on Pref@3 and Comb@3 for every centralized size**, and the best Comb@3 uses no explicit graph context. Section 7.1 acknowledges combined-context degradation. Section 7.3 compares selected best configurations with baselines and reports advantages on preference/joint hits, but exact graphical baseline differences were not digitized in the evidence notes. Nothing here shows that natural-language summaries outperform matched structured histories. ([§7.1–7.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15993v1#S7); [§8](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15993v1#S8))

FLARKO is the **nearest application prior work**, but it has no pre-cutoff sparse-cohort analysis. PKG/MKG/Nothing ablations change information, and the text does not fully establish identical users/candidate lists across ablations, exact hit-rate denominators, repeat-purchase policy or invalid/duplicate-ISIN handling. These are verification gaps, not evidence that cohorts definitely changed. Its original v1 main text and local PDF were read during the evidence research; the ICAIF publication metadata is verified separately, while the final publisher text was blocked. ([§§6–7](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15993v1#S6); [ACM-deposited metadata](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3768292.3770399))

## Language-profile studies expose benefits and information loss

### UPR supports editable review profiles, with competitive rather than superior ranking

Ramos, Rahmani, Wang, Fu and Lipani's **User Profile Recommendation (UPR)** extracts sentiment-bearing features from reviews, ranks them using rating-derived utility, coverage and significance, and prompts Llama2-7B or Mistral-7B to write a first-person profile. Defaults select **five features**, **five reviews per feature** and a **200-token** profile. A separately fine-tuned **GPT-2** predicts a rating from the profile and candidate item title. The generated profile is thus a compact, editable representation of explicit review sentiment, not a faithful serialization of transaction IDs, amounts and dates. ([ACL published PDF, §§3.2–3.4, 4.4; Appendix A](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.753.pdf))

Amazon Movies and TV contains **4,811 users, 5,459 items and 288,693 records**; TripAdvisor contains **9,765 users, 6,280 items and 320,023 records**. The **8:1:1** split requires every user/item to appear in every split and at least **five training reviews per user**; no global chronological cutoff is established. RMSE/MAE evaluate ratings; MAP and nDCG@10 use **only explicitly rated test items in condensed lists**. Consequently, values near 0.94 cannot be compared with catalog-wide FAR-Trans purchase nDCG. ([§4.1/Table 1; §4.2, p.13975](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.753.pdf))

Table 2 reports the following selected results. They are descriptive comparisons without a supplied equivalence test or uncertainty intervals for these pairs. ([Table 2, p.13977](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.753.pdf))

| Model | Amazon RMSE | Amazon nDCG@10 | TripAdvisor RMSE | TripAdvisor nDCG@10 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| MF | 0.925 | **0.941** | 0.786 | **0.960** |
| UPR (Llama2) | 0.944 | 0.938 | 0.804 | 0.955 |
| UPR (Mistral) | 0.941 | 0.940 | 0.804 | 0.952 |

Profile quality was assessed by **five master's-student annotators**, fifty profiles per domain; relevance satisfaction was **90%/87%** for Amazon/TripAdvisor. An editing experiment with **200 users** and lists of **100 feature-matching plus 100 other items** found higher target-feature Coverage@10 after positive profile edits. This supports readability and steering, not future-purchase accuracy or financial utility. The authors also report hallucinated/inferred content, review dependence, loss of rare preferences and difficulties with negative preferences. ([§5.3/Table 3, §5.4; §§6–8; Appendix A.5–A.6](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.753.pdf))

UPR belongs in the five-paper set because it concretely demonstrates the profile pipeline and its trade-offs. Its CF comparisons change architecture and available semantics, while the editing test intentionally adds preferences. Neither isolates the encoding of the same sparse history. Warm-start review selection and feature-count ablations are not pre-cutoff financial sparsity tests. ([§§3–5](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.753.pdf))

### LangPTune is the nearest methodological precedent and a necessary counterexample

The verified CIKM publication is **LangPTune: Optimizing Language-based User Profiles for Recommendation** by Gao, Zhou, Dai and Joachims. The experimental text read is the local **`2410.18870v2.pdf`**, titled **End-to-end Training for Recommendation with Language-based User Profiles**, dated **12 February 2025**. The initial preprint was submitted 24 October 2024; CIKM publication metadata dates to 10 November 2025. These are linked versions of the work, but final-table equivalence remains unchecked. ([Publication metadata](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3746252.3761369); [official accepted papers](https://cikm2025.org/program/accepted-papers); [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18870))

In v2, a **Gemma-2B-it or Llama-3-8B-Instruct** encoder generates a natural-language profile from historical item metadata and reviews. An **Mxbai embedding decoder** scores the profile against candidate metadata by inner product. **RLSO** optimizes the generator using downstream ranking rewards, while **contrastive learning (CL/InfoNCE)** trains the decoder; full LangPTune alternates both. This is task-optimized profile generation, not simply applying a fixed zero-shot summarizer. The variants freeze both components (-0), train only the decoder (-CL), or train only the generator (-RLSO). ([v2 §§3.1–3.2, 4.2–4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18870v2#S3))

Amazon Movie-TV and Books examples contain **four distinct historical items and one distinct future item**, after filtering sessions/items with fewer than five interactions and overly long metadata/histories. Movie-TV has **14,698/1,392/1,392** train/validation/test sessions and **10,533 items**; Books has **4,404/361/361** sessions and **4,819 items**. Metrics are **Recall@20, MRR@20 and NDCG@20**. The short inputs are relevant to the RQ, but are not naturally sparse FAR-Trans cohorts selected before a shared calendar cutoff. Baseline access to validation/test history items, permitted by footnote 1, further differs from a simple globally chronological split; that is not by itself evidence of future-target leakage. ([v2 §4.1/Table 1; §4.2/footnote 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18870v2#S4.T1))

Joint training improves several language-profile baselines: Llama/Movie-TV NDCG@20 increases from **0.0231** (-0) to **0.0494** (full); Gemma/Books increases from **0.1743** to **0.2736**. The reported **113.9%** and **57.0%** relative gains compare optimized with zero-shot profile pipelines, **not summaries with structured histories**. Full LangPTune is not uniformly best against conventional models: Movie-TV **0.0494** is below LightGCN **0.0525** and MCL **0.0563**, whereas Books **0.2736** exceeds LightGCN **0.2586**. ([v2 Tables 2–3](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18870v2#S4.T2))

The most relevant comparison bypasses profile generation: **NoProfile-0** directly embeds the historical item-metadata list with pretrained Mxbai; **NoProfile-CL** trains this direct-input decoder with InfoNCE. Table 4 gives the following **Movie-TV/Llama** results. ([v2 §4.5.1/Table 4, p.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18870v2#S4.T4))

| Pipeline | Maximum profile tokens | Recall@20 | MRR@20 | NDCG@20 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| LangPTune | 64 | 0.0604 | 0.0201 | 0.0319 |
| LangPTune | 128 | 0.0901 | 0.0313 | 0.0444 |
| LangPTune | 256 | 0.0929 | 0.0352 | 0.0482 |
| LangPTune | 512 | 0.0970 | 0.0358 | 0.0494 |
| NoProfile-0 | — | 0.0760 | 0.0261 | 0.0372 |
| **NoProfile-CL** | — | **0.1012** | **0.0408** | **0.0543** |

**The best direct-history comparator exceeds the 512-token profile by 0.0049 NDCG@20.** Equivalently, the profile is approximately 9.0% below NoProfile-CL, arithmetic derived from the table; this pair has no reported significance marker. The authors explicitly discuss the language-profile information bottleneck. A limited fixed-decoder comparison also exists: **NoProfile-0 0.0372**, **LangPTune-0 0.0231**, and **LangPTune-RLSO 0.0377** use the pretrained decoder on Movie-TV. This is relevant partial control evidence and should not be dismissed, but reviews, formatting/truncation parity and significance of these exact contrasts remain unresolved. Full LangPTune versus NoProfile-CL instead uses separately trained decoder weights and different training settings. ([v2 Tables 2/4, §§4.2.1, 4.5.1; Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18870v2))

Two other distinctions prevent overstatement. Reducing **global training-set size from 100% to 0.1%** is not a per-user sparsity experiment. The human interpretability test finds **58.89%** binary next-movie choice accuracy for trained profiles versus **57.11%** raw history and **54.67%** zero-shot profiles, with trained-versus-raw **p=0.624** and trained-versus-zero-shot **p=0.201**. Those nonsignificant comparisons and shorter reading times concern a readability/usefulness trade-off, not proof of superior ranking or faithful transaction summaries. ([v2 §4.5.2/Fig.4; §4.6/Tables 5–6](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18870v2#S4.SS6))

## Financial evaluation bounds the meaning of an eventual result

### Investors Are (Not) Always Right separates predicting purchases from earning returns

Sanz-Cruzado, McCreadie, Droukas, Macdonald and Ounis compare transaction-based and customer-independent profitability metrics theoretically and empirically. Theorem 5.6 establishes **zero correlation over all possible valid user–ranking pairs at a fixed time/horizon**, under its assumptions. The authors use “independence,” but zero covariance alone is not general statistical independence; the safe substantive conclusion is **no guaranteed positive relationship**, not a universal law about actual investors. ([TOIS §5.3, Theorem 5.6](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#sec-5-3))

The empirical study uses FAR-Trans and **twelve algorithms**, adding **customer profile similarity (CPS)** to the original eleven-method inventory. The main protocol has **61 temporal variants**, a six-month horizon, binary novel-acquisition relevance and test-activity/pricing filters. Hyperparameters are again selected for six-month ROI at the three dates described above. The article states origins from **1 August 2019 to end-November 2021**, spaced two weeks apart; those statements do not yield an unambiguous literal 61-origin schedule. A complete date list remains a reproduction question. CPS also uses demographic information that the article's Data Statement says could not be publicly shared. ([§6.1–6.3](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#sec-6); [Data Statement](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#sec-13))

Unlike the workshop paper, this article supplies explicit metric equations: binary purchase gain with logarithmic rank discount, an equally weighted top-k portfolio, and monthly conversion **`(1 + ROI)^(30 / horizon_days) − 1`**. The reported pooled correlation between **nDCG@10 and monthly ROI@10 is −0.13**, over approximately **1.5 million user × algorithm × temporal-variant values**, with authors reporting **p < 0.05**. This is not a correlation over only twelve model means. Repeated users and overlapping horizons mean that the pooled count should not be treated automatically as independent observations; the literature notes did not recalculate the uncertainty. ([§6.2/Eqs.7–12](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#sec-6-2); [§7/Fig.4, p.51:23](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#sec-7))

Table 5 repeats shared FAR-Trans baseline values, including **LightGCN 0.3404 nDCG / 0.0004 monthly ROI**, **popularity 0.2710 / 0.0006**, and **random forest 0.0237 / 0.0259**. CPS gives **0.2722 / 0.0026**. These are useful metric contrasts, not a second independent demonstration of representation gains. The article's synthetic effective-investor experiment changes the pooled correlation to **+0.13**; date-specific correlations vary approximately **−0.65 to +0.4**. Thus a universally negative empirical relation is also unsupported. ([§8.1/Table 5](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#tab5); [§9.2/Fig.7](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#sec-9-2); [§10/Fig.8](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#sec-10))

Limitations include one institution, partial portfolios, omitted dividend/coupon income, equal-allocation/customer-independent profitability and simplified common investment horizons. The authors explicitly say abandoning transaction-based evaluation is premature. For this RQ, improved nDCG@10 would mean **better ranking of future observed purchases**, not necessarily higher profits, suitability, risk reduction or welfare. The article contains no LLM-summary experiment or sparse-specific representation result. ([§12 and limitations](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3780097#sec-12))

### Conv-FinRe is an investigated alternative with a different target

**Conv-FinRe: A Conversational and Longitudinal Benchmark for Utility-Grounded Financial Recommendation** is verified as a **SIGIR 2026** publication. Experimental claims here use **arXiv v2**, a pre-camera-ready version. The publisher-deposited record has **thirteen authors**, while the arXiv version has **fourteen**, including **Rosie Guo** rather than the publication's **Yuqing Guo**, and **Xue Liu**, absent from the publication metadata. Their identities/reasons for the differences are not inferred; both full lists appear in [reference_verification.md](reference_verification.md#6-conv-finre--investigated-alternative). ([Publisher-deposited record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3805712.3808658); [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16990))

This benchmark combines real market prices with **ten human participants' simulator decisions**, not FAR-Trans brokerage purchases. Ten stocks and **23 steps per user** yield **230 prefix-conditioned instances**, **270 unique turns** and **3,450 prefix-expanded turns**. Onboarding and later dialogues are generated from profiles and decision trajectories, rather than collected natural human-advisor conversations. Models rerank the fixed stock set using profile/history, market and advisor information. Utility combines standardized seven-day return with individual volatility/drawdown penalties, whose parameters are fitted over each participant's **full longitudinal trajectory**. ([v2 §§3.1–3.3/Tables 2–3](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16990v2#S3))

Metrics include **utility-gain uNDCG over the full candidate set**, choice MRR/HR@1/HR@3 and Kendall τ against advisor rankings. Table 4 reports **Llama-3.3-70B uNDCG 0.97 ± 0.00**, while the finance-specific **XuanYuan3** has **MRR 0.65 ± 0.03** and **HR@1 0.54 ± 0.03**. The inspected caption/text does not clearly identify the ± estimator, so these are retained as reported uncertainty, not relabeled standard errors. History/no-history results are model-dependent: some improve, some barely change, and some lose utility alignment. Removing history changes information; it is not a summary-versus-structured contrast. ([v2 §3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16990v2#S3.SS4); [Table 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16990v2#S4.T4); [§4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16990v2#S4.SS3))

The small repeated-prefix sample, generated dialogue and retrospective utility calibration limit transfer. Full-trajectory fitting raises a point-in-time question if adapted to prospective prediction, without establishing a measured leakage effect in this diagnostic benchmark. The signed-utility equations do not document a nonnegative gain transformation, so conventional binary-nDCG bounds cannot be assumed; **0.97 is not comparable with FAR-Trans's 0.3404**. This paper is retained as adjacent evaluation context and excluded from the core five because Investors supplies more direct FAR-Trans metric evidence, while LangPTune better addresses the representation question. ([v2 §§3.2–3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16990v2#S3.SS2); [§5](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16990v2#S5))

## The preparation supports a bounded, two-sided research gap

**Among the studies inspected, we did not find a same-information comparison of LLM-generated transaction-history summaries versus structured-history features for a pre-cutoff sparse FAR-Trans cohort, evaluated on future-purchase nDCG@10 under matched users, candidates and downstream ranking conditions.** LangPTune is the closest methodological precedent because it tests bypassing the profile generator, including frozen-decoder variants; FLARKO is closest in dataset/application. Neither establishes the requested financial effect. UPR's lower nDCG than MF, LangPTune's direct-history advantage and FLARKO's mixed context results make a negative or null outcome scientifically informative. No expected effect size can be transferred across their different relevance definitions, cutoffs and candidate sets. ([LangPTune v2 Table 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18870v2#S4.T4); [FLARKO Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15993v1#S7.T2); [UPR Table 2](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.753.pdf))

The remaining preparation task is to clarify the permitted comparison once the class protocol is available: historical information, sparse counting unit, new versus repeat purchases, candidate availability and what “fixed ranker” means. [research_gap.md](research_gap.md) records these as **pending controls**, not an approved experiment or an invented official split.
